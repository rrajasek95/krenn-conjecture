# Algebraic literature and attribution review

This supplements `algebra-review.md`. It concerns the algebraic tools and attribution in the September 26 reviewed baseline, rather than certifying the full conjecture proof. Sources were checked on September 26, 2026. No manuscript edits were made in preparing this report.

**Main finding:** the existing Isserlis and Kraft–Procesi bibliographic references are accurate. They support standard ingredients, not the whole source-specific argument. The important attribution boundary is between the classical scalar orthogonal invariant theorem and the manuscript's more restrictive matrix identity. The latter also needs the divisibility information derived from the matching equations.

## 1. Pairing sums: standard formula, explicitly algebraic use

The existing four-factor display is the right entry point. Attribute the pairing formula to Isserlis precisely, then tell readers that the paper **defines** a polynomial functional by that formula. The graph argument is not an application of a theorem asserting the existence of a random vector with the stated complex coefficients.

Suggested manuscript wording:

> We use the pairing polynomial appearing in Isserlis's moment formula [§2, equations (5)–(6)] as an algebraic definition. Given any symmetric family of coefficients, the functional is defined on monomials by summing products over all pairings and extended linearly. No probability measure is introduced.

The extension to arbitrary complex coefficients, including singular coefficient matrices, is immediate from this definition: each requested moment is a finite polynomial in those coefficients. There is no inverse covariance in that formula. This short explanation is preferable to invoking analytic continuation of a Gaussian integral.

Hamilton–Lazarev is a genuinely relevant optional supplementary reference for readers wanting an explicitly algebraic contraction viewpoint. Its scope should be identified rather than imported wholesale. It does not supply the manuscript's GHZ-specific response identities or its matching rigidity conclusion. One optional sentence and citation in an appendix is sufficient; adding a discussion of graph cohomology or BV quantization to the introduction would be distracting.

If the term “Wick” remains prominent, the 1950 Wick reference can be added for historical terminology. Isserlis is already the more direct reference for the commutative scalar pairing formula actually used. There is no need to introduce operator normal ordering merely to justify the name.

**Terminology repair:** “Wick contraction” should mean one factor of the assigned symmetric bilinear pairing, or a specified pairing of symbol occurrences, as explicitly declared. It should not silently refer to a quantum expectation value, a tensor-network edge contraction, and a Gaussian covariance interchangeably.

## 2. Two copies: a standard symmetry and a source-specific application

The basic two-copy invariance needs a transparent calculation more than an additional theorem citation. In the notation of the first review, the pairing matrix on the copy index is \(I_2\), so the complete pairing table is \(I_2\otimes C\). Mixing copy labels by \(O\), with \(O^TO=I_2\), preserves it. This remains true when \(C\) is singular.

This observation establishes the following distinction for the contribution statement:

- **Standard algebra:** the finite pairing formula depends only on pairwise coefficients; a linear transformation preserving those coefficients preserves its moments; a two-by-two determinant is multiplied by \(\det O\).
- **What this manuscript must establish from its source equations:** the choice of the auxiliary symbols and reflection, the resulting coefficient identity, the common response factors, their binary mixed-coefficient vanishing, and the finite polynomial equation that removes all positive higher responses.

It would be inappropriate to claim the bare act of duplicating variables or preserving a block-diagonal pairing as a new theorem. Conversely, a classical covariance reference does not eliminate the need to prove the response identities produced by the particular construction.

Avoid suggesting that this is a probabilistic “replica method” or a characterization of Gaussian distributions. The manuscript has a fixed number of algebraic copies, no averaging over disorder, and no continuation in the number of copies. Those associations would create a literature obligation without explaining the proof.

## 3. Orthogonal invariant theory: what the citation does and does not prove

The Kraft–Procesi theorem applies over the complex numbers to the standard symmetric **bilinear** form. For two vectors, its scalar generators specialize to the three Gram coordinates \(P\cdot P\), \(Q\cdot Q\), and \(P\cdot Q\). The manuscript should attach this citation only to the assertion that the already identified scalar invariant coefficients are polynomials in these coordinates. The theorem's generator statement alone does not assert algebraic independence; that additional fact can be justified by the manuscript's direct two-vector calculation.

The stronger matrix expression

\[
T(P,Q)=a(P,Q)I_2+b(P,Q)PQ^T
\]

does **not** follow from orthogonal covariance alone. This is an elementary distinction visible without any literature search. The polynomial matrix

\[
T(P,Q)=PP^T
\]

also transforms by \(T(OP,OQ)=OT(P,Q)O^T\). But with \(P=(1,0)^T\) and \(Q=(0,1)^T\), its diagonal entries are 1 and 0, whereas \(aI_2+bPQ^T\) has equal diagonal entries. Thus the special source-dependent divisibility conditions are essential.

Suggested manuscript wording immediately before the normal-form lemma:

> Orthogonal covariance alone permits more matrix terms. The vanishing identities derived from the original matching equations also force the off-diagonal entries to contain specified parameter factors. We use those divisibilities to reduce the matrix to an identity term and one rank-one term. The remaining scalar coefficients are then controlled by the classical orthogonal invariant theorem.

Suggested wording at the actual citation:

> These scalar coefficients are invariant under \(\mathrm O(2,\mathbb C)\). The first fundamental theorem for the orthogonal group [Kraft–Procesi, §10.2(a), p.114] therefore expresses them as polynomials in the three Gram coordinates. We include an elementary proof of this two-vector specialization.

Do not title the entire matrix restriction “the first fundamental theorem,” “an immediate FFT consequence,” or a general classification of orthogonally covariant matrices. The present source-dependent lemma is more specific than those phrases suggest. The existing baseline citation is already attached to the scalar assertion; preserve that distinction when reorganizing the proof.

## 4. Polarization: cite or define the exact operation, not just its name

Kraft–Procesi §4.4 defines full polarization by extracting the coefficient of \(t_1\cdots t_d\) from \(f(\sum_i t_i v_i)\); §4.5 records the factorial recovered on the diagonal. These are precisely the conventions needed here.

For our manuscript, the operational explanation is short enough to include in the main text:

> Substitute \(L=t_1L_1+\cdots+t_pL_p\) into the homogeneous vanishing identity and take the coefficient of \(t_1\cdots t_p\). This coefficient is \(p!\) times the corresponding expression with the arbitrary rows \(L_1,\ldots,L_p\); because we work over \(\mathbb C\), the factorial can be cancelled.

Add `\cite[Sections~4.4--4.5]{kraft}` at the first occurrence if a background citation is wanted. No new bibliography item is necessary. The argument is a standard conversion from a diagonal homogeneous polynomial identity to its symmetric multilinear form; the source-specific work is proving the original vanishing identity on the correct row space.

## 5. Polynomial-degree rigidity: elementary proof, restrained naming

Both of the following are direct algebraic degree comparisons:

\[
g(L/\sqrt2)^2=g(L),\quad g(0)=1,
\]

and, for \(d\ne0\) in an integral domain,

\[
p'+dp=k.
\]

The first compares the total degree of a nonzero polynomial with twice that degree. In the second, the derivative cannot cancel the highest-degree term contributed by \(dp\). These arguments are already proved in the manuscript. They do not require citing an advanced differential-equation theorem, a positivity theorem, or a named algebraic existence result.

My recommendation is to call the second lemma **“A finite-degree observation”** or **“Polynomial solutions of a first-order identity.”** Explain that the important constraint is polynomiality: general analytic solutions can have exponential terms, so the conclusion comes from the finite matching expansion. The manuscript's mathematical contribution is the derivation of these particular identities from its matching symmetries; it should not present the leading-coefficient argument itself as a new piece of ODE theory.

I did not find it necessary to attach an additional primary citation to this elementary, fully written argument. This is an editorial assessment, not a claim to have established the first historical occurrence of either observation.

“Source-specific” in this table means that these claims depend on the particular matching setup and are the work the manuscript performs. It is not a blanket assertion of historical novelty across all mathematical literature. The paper can identify its construction precisely without making unverifiable priority claims about every algebraic substep.

## 7. Presentation lessons from the checked technical sources

Isserlis's first page is directly relevant to our opening of the pairing section: it establishes the four-variable case before introducing the general pairing rule. Our manuscript should continue that example through the two-copy construction, instead of immediately changing the index set and object types. [Isserlis, p.134](https://academic.oup.com/biomet/article-abstract/12/1-2/134/193428)

The useful lesson from the invariant-theory reference is to declare the field, vector space, bilinear form, and action before stating what is invariant. Our revised section should follow that order and explicitly distinguish the scalar polynomial \(G\) from its covariant matrix of derivatives. This is a recommendation for our exposition, not a request to reproduce a textbook's general theory.

Hamilton–Lazarev explicitly distinguishes standard background from its application and includes the finite setting it needs instead of requiring readers to import a more general framework. Its introduction and §4 provide an appropriate model for our attribution paragraph: acknowledge the classical ingredients, explain the limited machinery actually used, and state which construction the manuscript contributes. Its BV and superspace machinery should not be imported into ours. [Authors' preprint, introduction and §4](https://archive.mpim-bonn.mpg.de/739/1/preprint_2007_15.pdf)

## Recommended concrete changes

1. Keep the two existing bibliography entries; their metadata and the cited FFT location checked out.
2. Pinpoint Isserlis §2, equations (5)–(6), at the general pairing formula and explicitly call its use here an algebraic definition.
3. Add a precise polarization explanation and, if desired, a section-level citation to the already listed Kraft–Procesi reference.
4. Introduce the matrix normal form as the combination of **source-derived divisibility** and **classical scalar invariant theory**, not as a ready-made invariant-theory theorem.
5. Use an optional modern formal-Wick reference only if it helps readers; one qualified sentence suffices. Do not expand the bibliography with superficially related QFT techniques.
6. Add a short contribution paragraph explaining that the novelty asserted for this proof is the matching-specific construction and deduction, while its pairing combinatorics, group invariants, polarization, and degree comparisons are standard tools.

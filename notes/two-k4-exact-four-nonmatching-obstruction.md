# Exact-four singular support must be a matching

## 1. Result

Consider the two-`K_4` chart with standard ternary equality tensors on both
shores.  Assume that exactly four of the sixteen cross blocks are singular.

**Theorem 1.1.**  The four singular positions form a transversal perfect
matching.

In particular, the `3`-row/`3`-column orbit consisting of a bipartite
`P_4` and a disjoint edge is impossible.  The proof also excludes the two
other spread nonmatching orbits.  Its new input is a one-defect extension
of the six-cell Hessian-erasure lemma: one star may have an arbitrary
component at one site, provided its other three components and every
component of the first star are invertible.

The exact checker is
[`verify_two_k4_exact_four_nonmatching_obstruction.py`](../computations/verify_two_k4_exact_four_nonmatching_obstruction.py).

## 2. One-defect six-cell erasure

Let

\[
 \mathcal R=\bigotimes_{i=0}^3(\mathbb F\oplus V_i),
 \qquad V_i^2=0,\qquad \dim V_i=3,                    \tag{1}
\]

over a field of characteristic different from two.  Let `U,W` be
three-spaces, let every `P_i:U -> V_i` be an isomorphism, and suppose that
three of the four maps `S_i:W -> V_i` are isomorphisms.  The fourth `S_i`
is arbitrary.  Put

\[
 p_\alpha=\sum_iP_i\alpha,\qquad
 s_\beta=\sum_iS_i\beta.                              \tag{2}
\]

**Lemma 2.1 (one-defect erasure).**  If `U_0` is a two-plane and
`q in R_2` satisfies

\[
                  q p_\alpha s_\beta=0
       \qquad(\alpha\in U_0,\ \beta\in W),             \tag{3}
\]

then `q=0`.

Thus six zero cells in the pulled-back `3 by 3` pair-Hessian table still
determine the quadratic when one component of the second star is singular,
or even zero.

## 3. The almost-invertible-star annihilator

Relabel the exceptional site as zero and normalize

\[
                    S_1=S_2=S_3=I,\qquad S_0=A.        \tag{4}
\]

For a cubic `T`, write `T_i` for its component missing site `i`.  The
coefficient equations for `T s_\beta=0` are

\[
 A_{a_0b}T_0(a_1,a_2,a_3)
 +\sum_{i=1}^3 {\bf1}_{a_i=b}T_i(a_{\widehat i})=0.     \tag{5}
\]

Independent basis changes reduce `A` to
`diag(I_r,0)`.  Direct comparison in (5) gives

\[
\begin{array}{c|cccc}
\operatorname {rank}A&0&1&2&3\\ \hline
\dim\{T:T s_\beta=0\}&27&1&1&1.
\end{array}                                             \tag{6}
\]

For `A=0`, the annihilator consists exactly of arbitrary tensors `T_0` on
sites `1,2,3`; all other hole components vanish.  For `A!=0`, it is
generated by

\[
\begin{aligned}
 T_0(a_1,a_2,a_3)&=\epsilon_{a_1a_2a_3},\\
 T_1(a_0,a_2,a_3)&=-\sum_dA_{a_0d}\epsilon_{da_2a_3},\\
 T_2(a_0,a_1,a_3)&= \sum_dA_{a_0d}\epsilon_{da_1a_3},\\
 T_3(a_0,a_1,a_2)&=-\sum_dA_{a_0d}\epsilon_{da_1a_2}.
                                                               \tag{7}
\end{aligned}
\]

Substitution proves that (7) solves (5); the same coefficient comparison
that gives (6) proves uniqueness.  Crucially, the hole-zero component is
the full determinant tensor and has mode rank three, independently of the
rank of nonzero `A`.

## 4. Proof of the erasure lemma

For `alpha in U_0`, set `T_alpha=q p_alpha`.  Equation (3) puts every
`T_alpha` in the annihilator (6).

First suppose `A!=0`.  The map `alpha mapsto T_alpha` goes from a
two-plane to the one-dimensional line (7), so choose nonzero `alpha` with

\[
                              q p_\alpha=0.              \tag{8}
\]

The full-support multiplication lemma gives

\[
 q_{ij}=z_{ij}p_i\otimes p_j,qquad
 z_{ij}+z_{ik}+z_{jk}=0,                                \tag{9}
\]

where `p_i=P_i alpha`.  Choose `alpha'` independent of `alpha` in `U_0`.
The hole-zero component of `q p_(alpha')` is supported in the local
two-planes

\[
                     \operatorname {span}(P_i\alpha,P_i\alpha')
                          \quad(i=1,2,3),                \tag{10}
\]

so every mode rank is at most two.  A nonzero member of (7) has mode rank
three there.  Hence `q p_(alpha')=0`.  In each three-site component, its
three terms put `P_i alpha'` at three distinct sites and are linearly
independent.  Therefore every `z_ij` is zero and `q=0`.

It remains to handle `A=0`.  Now (6) says that every component of
`q p_alpha` on a triple containing site zero vanishes.  We use the
following elementary three-site fact.

**Lemma 4.1 (two-plane triangle syzygy).**  On three sites, if

\[
 q_{ij}P_k\alpha+q_{ik}P_j\alpha+q_{jk}P_i\alpha=0
                         \qquad(\alpha\in U_0),          \tag{11}
\]

then, for an ordered basis `e,f` of `U_0`, the three blocks are a common
scalar multiple, with alternating boundary signs, of

\[
 \omega_{ab}=P_ae\otimes P_bf-P_af\otimes P_be.         \tag{12}
\]

Indeed, normalize the two local plane directions to colors `1,2`.
Coefficient comparison kills 26 of the 27 block coordinates; the survivor
is

\[
                 (q_{ij},q_{ik},q_{jk})
                         =\lambda(\omega_{ij},-\omega_{ik},\omega_{jk}).
                                                               \tag{13}
\]

Apply (13) to the triples `012`, `013`, and `023`.  The shared block
`q_01` makes the first two scalars equal, say to `lambda`.  Comparing
`q_02` makes the third scalar `-lambda`, while comparing `q_03` then gives
`lambda=-lambda`.  Characteristic different from two forces `lambda=0`.
All six blocks of `q` vanish.  This proves Lemma 2.1.

## 5. The mixed two-/four-cross equation

Suppose one cross-block row `r` is completely invertible and another row
`s` contains at most one singular block.  Let `0,t` be the complementary
left vertices and put `c=kappa(0t)`.  On the four right sites define

\[
 p_{i,x}=\sum_j\operatorname {row}_x(B_{ij})^{(j)},
 \qquad q_{\rm eff}=q_R+p_{0,c}p_{t,c}.                 \tag{14}
\]

For a left word having color `c` at `0,t` and colors `x,y` at `r,s`, the
complete sector using the internal edge `0t` is

\[
                         q_{\rm eff}p_{r,x}p_{s,y}.      \tag{15}
\]

When `(x,y)!=(c,c)`, this is the unique compatible left internal edge; the
word is nonconstant, so both the target coefficient and the zero-cross
term vanish.  In particular,

\[
                  q_{\rm eff}p_{r,x}p_{s,y}=0
                  \qquad(x\ne c,\ y=0,1,2).             \tag{16}
\]

The row maps of `r` are four isomorphisms.  Three row maps of `s` are
isomorphisms and the fourth is arbitrary.  Lemma 2.1 applied to the two
input rows `x!=c` forces `q_eff=0`.

This is impossible.  If `q_R=-p_(0,c)p_(t,c)`, then at each right site all
three endpoint lines of the incident right-`K_4` blocks lie in the plane
spanned by the two local factors of `p_(0,c)` and `p_(t,c)`.  Those three
endpoint lines are the three distinct coordinate axes, which span dimension
three.  We have proved:

**Proposition 5.1.**  No solution has one completely invertible block row
and a second block row with at most one singular block.  The transposed
statement holds for block columns.

## 6. Exact-four consequence

The preceding six-cell obstruction already shows that singular positions
meet at least three block rows and three block columns.  With four singular
positions, a support that misses one row has row-degree partition

\[
                              (2,1,1,0).                 \tag{17}
\]

Its degree-zero row and either degree-one row contradict Proposition 5.1.
Thus all four block rows are met exactly once.  Transposition gives the same
statement for columns.  The singular positions therefore form a
transversal perfect matching, proving Theorem 1.1.

For the requested `P_4` plus disjoint-edge orbit, normalize the positions to

\[
                       (0,0),(0,1),(1,0),(2,2).          \tag{18}
\]

Row `3` is completely invertible and row `2` has only the exceptional block
`B_22`, so Proposition 5.1 excludes it directly.

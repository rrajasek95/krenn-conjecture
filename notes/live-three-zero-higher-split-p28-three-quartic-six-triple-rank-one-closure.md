# The \(p=28\) \(4^3 3^6\) residual rank-one branch is impossible

## 1. Result and scope

Continue from the independently audited
[\(q=5\) saturation theorem](live-three-zero-higher-split-p28-three-quartic-six-triple-q5-saturation.md).
Thus the common kernel is a six-space

\[
                    {\cal K}\subseteq\mathbb C[z]_{\leq9}
\]

with echelon degrees \(4,5,6,7,8,9\).  Its three exact quartic rows and
six exact triple rows consume the whole Wronskian degree

\[
                       3(6-4)+6(6-3)=24.
\]

Write a basis evaluation vector as

\[
              F(z)=E(t)+zO(t),\qquad t=z^2,
\]

and let

\[
 W_t=\langle E,O,E',O'\rangle,\qquad
 {\cal A}_t=W_t^\perp.
\]

The residual four-wedge is nonzero, and the primitive annihilator bundle
has splitting

\[
 {\cal A}\simeq {\cal O}(-\alpha)\oplus{\cal O}(-\beta),
 \qquad 2\leq\alpha\leq\beta,\qquad
 d=\alpha+\beta\leq6.                                      \tag{1}
\]

**Theorem 1.1.**  The second fundamental map of \(W\) cannot have generic
rank one.

Consequently the only residual branches left by the saturation theorem
are the generic-rank-two splittings \((2,4)\) and \((3,3)\).  This note
does not close either of those two branches and hence is not by itself a
closure of the \(4^3 3^6\) profile.

## 2. From rank one to a tangent-edge curve

The second fundamental map of \(W\) is dual to the tangent map of the
line curve

\[
                  \mathbb P({\cal A}_t)\subset\mathbb P(V^*),
                  \qquad \dim V=6.
\]

If its generic rank is one, the swept ruled surface is developable.  In
characteristic zero, saturation gives the usual dichotomy: the lines
either pass through one fixed vertex, or they are the tangent lines to a
nonconstant edge curve \(\gamma\).

The cone case is impossible.  A fixed projective vertex gives a nowhere
zero constant section \({\cal O}\to{\cal A}\), whereas (1) has no global
section.  We may therefore write, locally and then after saturation,

\[
                         {\cal A}_t=\langle\gamma(t),
                                                \gamma'(t)\rangle. \tag{2}
\]

Let \(U\subseteq V^*\) be the coefficient span of \(\gamma\), put
\(m=\dim U\), let \(e\) be the degree of the nondegenerate edge in
\(\mathbb P(U)\), and set

\[
                 R_1=\sum_x(a_1(x)-1).
\]

The tangent-line degree and the Pluecker degree of \({\cal A}\) agree:

\[
                         d=2e-2-R_1.                       \tag{3}
\]

The total ramification of the nondegenerate \(g^{m-1}_e\) is
\(m(e-m+1)\).  One unit of \(R_1\) raises at least the last \(m-1\)
orders, so

\[
                  (m-1)R_1\leq m(e-m+1).                  \tag{4}
\]

Since \(4\leq d\leq6\), \(e\geq m-1\), and \(m\leq6\), equations
(3)--(4) leave

\[
\begin{array}{c|c|c|c}
m&e&d&R_1\\ \hline
4&3&4&0\\
4&4&5,6&1,0\\
4&5&6&2\\
4&6&6&4\\
5&4&6&0.
\end{array}                                                \tag{5}
\]

For \(m\leq2\), the tangent line is constant.  For \(m=3\), the
three-space

\[
       \ker\langle\gamma,\gamma',\gamma''\rangle=U^\perp
\]

is fixed.  The identities below put both \(E\) and \(O\) in this fixed
space, hence also \(E',O'\), contradicting the nonzero four-wedge.  The
inequality (4) excludes \(m=6\).  Thus (5) is exhaustive.

Indeed, every member of \({\cal A}_t\) annihilates
\(E,O,E',O'\).  Differentiating \(\lambda E=0\) and using
\(\lambda E'=0\), and similarly for the other three combinations, gives

\[
 E(t),O(t)\in
 D_t:=\ker\langle\gamma,\gamma',\gamma''\rangle.           \tag{6}
\]

## 3. Four-dimensional edge span

Assume \(m=4\).  Choose a complement to the fixed two-space
\(C=U^\perp\subset V\).  In that complement, the kernel in (6) is a
line generated by the osculating-dual curve \(\nu(t)\):

\[
                         D_t=C\oplus\langle\nu(t)\rangle.   \tag{7}
\]

The curve \(\nu\) spans \(\mathbb P^3\).  One direct way to see this is
biduality: \(\gamma(t)\) is the annihilator of
\(\langle\nu,\nu',\nu''\rangle\).  If \(\nu\) lay in a proper fixed
projective subspace, this annihilator would make \(\gamma\) constant or
degenerate.

Let \(e^*=\deg\nu\).  Homogenize \(E,O\), whose coordinates have degree
at most four.  Their projections to the moving line in (7) are bundle
maps

\[
                 {\cal O}(-4)\longrightarrow{\cal O}(-e^*). \tag{8}
\]

At least one map is nonzero, since otherwise \(E,O\) and their
derivatives would all lie in the fixed two-space \(C\).  Therefore
\(e^*\leq4\).  In affine notation (6)--(8) have the form

\[
\begin{aligned}
 E(t)&=p(t)\nu(t)+c_E(t),\\
 O(t)&=q(t)\nu(t)+c_O(t),\qquad c_E,c_O\in C[t],
\end{aligned}                                               \tag{9}
\]

where \(p,q\) are the corresponding homogeneous scalar sections.  Put

\[
                         A(z)=p(z^2)+zq(z^2).               \tag{10}
\]

It is nonzero.  The four coordinate polynomials of the moving component
of \(F\) form

\[
                         A(z)\,{\cal V}(z^2),               \tag{11}
\]

where \({\cal V}\) is the nondegenerate four-dimensional coordinate
series of \(\nu\).

At the fixed square-cover branch point \(t=z=0\), the vanishing sequence
of \({\cal V}\) has last order at least three.  Hence (11) contains a
nonzero polynomial of order at least six at \(z=0\).  The six-dimensional
space \({\cal K}\) consequently has last vanishing order at least six,
so its Wronskian has positive order there.

## 4. Five-dimensional edge span

The sole \(m=5\) case in (5) has \(e=4\) and no ramification.  Thus the
edge is the rational normal quartic.  A constant target change, without
changing the fixed parameter \(t\), gives

\[
                         \gamma=(1,t,t^2,t^3,t^4).          \tag{12}
\]

Inside its five-dimensional coefficient span, the kernel of
\(\gamma,\gamma',\gamma''\) has the polynomial frame

\[
\begin{aligned}
 u&=(-t^3,3t^2,-3t,1,0),\\
 v&=(0,-t^3,3t^2,-3t,1).                                  \tag{13}
\end{aligned}

Together with the fixed line \(C=U^\perp\), equation (6) says

\[
 E=a(t)u+b(t)v+c_E(t),\qquad
 O=r(t)u+s(t)v+c_O(t).                                    \tag{14}
\]

The first and last coordinates in (13), followed by the second one,
show directly that every scalar \(a,b,r,s\) has degree at most one when
the coordinates of \(E,O\) have degree at most four.  In particular the
first coordinate of \(F\) is

\[
                    -z^6\bigl(a(z^2)+zr(z^2)\bigr).        \tag{15}
\]

It cannot vanish identically: that would require \(a=r=0\), and then the
first coordinate of the whole evaluation vector would be zero, giving a
constant relation among the six basis polynomials.  Thus (15) is a
nonzero member of \({\cal K}\) of order at least six at \(z=0\).

## 5. Contradiction with saturation

For a six-dimensional polynomial series, the existence of a nonzero
section of order at least six forces a vanishing sequence whose last term
is at least six.  Since its six terms are strictly increasing nonnegative
integers, the Wronskian weight is at least one.

Sections 3 and 4 therefore force

\[
                   \operatorname{ord}_{z=0}
                   \operatorname{Wr}({\cal K})\geq1.       \tag{16}
\]

But all three quartic and six triple values are nonzero, and their exact
weights already total twenty-four, the full Wronskian degree.  Thus zero
is an unlisted ramification point.  Equation (16) is impossible, proving
Theorem 1.1.

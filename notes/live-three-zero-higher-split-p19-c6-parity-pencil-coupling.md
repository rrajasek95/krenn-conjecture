# Higher splits: the \(p=19\), \(C=6\) parity-pencil coupling

## 1. Result

Continue on the exact \(p=19\) boundary after the three common-lift
closures.  Fix a moving-singleton selection for which the formal
complement has \(C=6\) value classes outside the moving pool.  Let \(P\)
be the size of that pool.

**Theorem 1.1 (small-pool closure).**  Every such profile with

\[
                              2\leq P\leq5                       \tag{1}
\]

is impossible.

This closes six of the twelve residual \(C=6\) families:

\[
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
 &3^3 2^5 1^{h+2},\qquad 3^4 2^4 1^{h+1},\qquad
   3^6 1^{h+3},\qquad 3^6 2\,1^{h+1},\\
 &4\,3\,2^6 1^{h+2},\qquad
   4\,3^2 2^5 1^{h+1}.
\end{aligned}}                                                \tag{2}
\]

The combined \(p=19\) ledger is therefore \(81/94\), with thirteen
families left.

The equality square pencils from different fixed moving-singleton choices
also have a uniform global consequence.

**Theorem 1.2 (global parity-rank collapse).**  For any hypothetical
\(C=6\) profile with \(P\geq6\), let \({\cal K}\) be the common
moving-singleton kernel.  After removing the forced pool factor, the
global parity map

\[
 \Phi_{\cal K}:\bigwedge\nolimits^2{\cal K}
       \longrightarrow \mathbb C[x]_{\leq3}                   \tag{3}
\]

has rank at most three.

Thus the saturated square pencils cannot be treated independently.  They
force the quotient Plücker curve into a projective plane section of the
Klein quadric.  This is the exact next normal form for the remaining
pool sizes \(P=6,7,8\).

## 2. The common four-space

For \(q\) in the pool, the selected relation space is

\[
 {\cal S}_q\subseteq\mathbb C[z]_{\leq n},\qquad
 \dim{\cal S}_q=3,\qquad n=P+1.                               \tag{4}
\]

The exact moving-singleton factor

\[
                         f_q=(z-q)^2(z+q)                      \tag{5}
\]

puts

\[
 {\cal T}_q=f_q{\cal S}_q\subseteq{\cal K}
       \subseteq\mathbb C[z]_{\leq N},\qquad N=P+4.            \tag{6}
\]

The five-capped-mass argument in the singleton-parity theorem gives
\(\dim{\cal K}\leq4\).  If \(P\geq2\), dimension three is already
impossible: it would make every \({\cal T}_q\) equal to \({\cal K}\), so
all of \({\cal K}\) would be divisible by the product of the pairwise
coprime cubics \(f_q\).  The resulting degree-\(N\) multiple space has
dimension less than three.  Therefore

\[
                             \dim{\cal K}=4.                    \tag{7}
\]

Each \({\cal T}_q\) is a hyperplane in this common four-space.

## 3. Pools of size two through four

For distinct pool values \(q_1,\ldots,q_s\), every member of

\[
                   {\cal T}_{q_1}\cap\cdots\cap{\cal T}_{q_s}  \tag{8}
\]

is divisible by \(f_{q_1}\cdots f_{q_s}\).  Two hyperplanes in a
four-space meet in at least a plane, while three meet in at least a line.

If \(P=2\), the ambient space of degree-\(N=6\) multiples of
\(f_{q_1}f_{q_2}\) is only one-dimensional, contradicting the
two-dimensional lower bound.  If \(P=3\) or \(4\), a product of three
cubics has degree nine, larger than \(N=P+4\), contradicting the
nonzero triple intersection.  This proves Theorem 1.1 for \(P\leq4\).

## 4. The five-pool complete triple graph

Let \(P=5\), so \(N=9\).  Every three moving hyperplanes have a nonzero
intersection, and the full ambient intersection of their cubic-multiple
spaces is the line generated by their degree-nine product.  Hence

\[
                  f_i f_j f_k\in{\cal K}                       \tag{9}
\]

for every triple of distinct pool values.

Fix a tested pool value \(v\), and let \(\Omega\) be the other four
values.  The common baseline row at \(v\) is

\[
                         J_v(T)=(U_vT)'(v),\qquad U_v(v)\ne0.  \tag{10}
\]

For every three-set \(I\subset\Omega\), equations (9)--(10) give

\[
 {U_v'(v)\over U_v(v)}
       +\sum_{x\in I}\left({2\over v-x}+{1\over v+x}\right)=0. \tag{11}
\]

Subtract the equations for two triples differing in one member.  All four
numbers

\[
                  \varphi_v(x)={2\over v-x}+{1\over v+x}
                         ={3v+x\over v^2-x^2}                  \tag{12}
\]

must be equal.  Every fibre of (12) has at most two structurally allowed
values: \(\varphi_v(x)=a\) is

\[
                         a(v^2-x^2)-(3v+x)=0,                  \tag{13}
\]

a nonzero polynomial of degree at most two whose coefficient of \(x\) is
\(-1\).  Four distinct pool values cannot lie in one fibre.  This closes
\(P=5\) and proves Theorem 1.1.

## 5. The saturated square pencil for one fixed choice

Fix \(q\).  For every other pool value \(t\), the two hyperplanes
\({\cal T}_q,{\cal T}_t\) meet in a plane.  Dividing by \(f_q\) gives

\[
 \dim\bigl({\cal S}_q\cap f_t\mathbb C[z]\bigr)\geq2
                  \qquad(t\ne q).                              \tag{14}
\]

There are \(m=P-1=n-2\) such values.  For \(A,B\in{\cal S}_q\), the odd
parity determinant

\[
                  A(z)B(-z)-A(-z)B(z)                          \tag{15}
\]

is divisible by one fixed odd polynomial of degree \(2m+1=2n-3\).
After division, the quotient is even of degree at most two.  Thus the
parity map from the three-dimensional space
\(\bigwedge^2{\cal S}_q\) has a nonzero kernel.  Every nonzero two-vector
in the exterior square of a three-space is decomposable, so there is a
two-dimensional **square pencil**

\[
                         {\cal L}_q\subseteq{\cal S}_q          \tag{16}
\]

whose every parity determinant vanishes.  Equivalently, after its gcd is
removed, \({\cal L}_q\) is a pencil in \(z^2\).

Multiplication by \(f_q\) gives a square pencil

\[
                  \widehat{\cal L}_q=f_q{\cal L}_q
                               \subseteq{\cal K}.               \tag{17}
\]

These are the pencils that must be compared across different fixed
choices \(q\).

## 6. One global parity map

For \(A,B\in{\cal K}\), every pool hyperplane \({\cal T}_q\) is contained
in both evaluation kernels at \(q\) and \(-q\).  Hence those two
evaluations are proportional on \({\cal K}\), and the parity determinant
of \(A,B\) vanishes at both signs of every nonzero pool value.

If zero is not in the pool, put

\[
                 H_P(z)=z\prod_q(z^2-q^2).                    \tag{18}
\]

If zero is in the pool, replace its factor by \(z^3\); the cubic
divisibility of the hyperplane \({\cal T}_0\) gives order at least three
at zero.  In both cases \(\deg H_P=2P+1\).  Since

\[
                 \deg\bigl(A(z)B(-z)-A(-z)B(z)\bigr)
                       \leq2N-1=2P+7,                          \tag{19}
\]

division by \(H_P\) gives an even polynomial of degree at most six, or a
polynomial of degree at most three in \(x=z^2\).  This is the global map
(3).  Every lifted pencil (17) determines a decomposable point of

\[
        \mathbb P(\ker\Phi_{\cal K})\cap\operatorname{Gr}(2,{\cal K})
          \subseteq\mathbb P\!\left(\bigwedge\nolimits^2{\cal K}\right).
                                                                    \tag{20}
\]

## 7. Comparing all fixed choices

Suppose, contrary to Theorem 1.2, that \(\Phi_{\cal K}\) had rank four.
Then \(\ker\Phi_{\cal K}\) would be two-dimensional, hence its
projectivization would be a line in \(\mathbb P^5\).

If that line were contained in the Klein quadric
\(\operatorname{Gr}(2,4)\), the standard flag description of a line in
the Grassmannian would make all its pencils share one nonzero section.
That section would belong to every \(\widehat{\cal L}_q\), hence would be
divisible by \(\prod_q f_q\), of degree \(3P>N\).  This is impossible.
Therefore the kernel line meets the Klein quadric in at most two points:
there are at most two distinct lifted square pencils.

One lifted square pencil cannot be repeated for too many pool values.  If
it equals \(\widehat{\cal L}_q\) for \(s\) distinct values \(q\), its gcd
contains their \(s\) coprime cubics.  A parity-zero pencil, after its gcd
is removed, is a two-space in \(z^2\), and therefore needs residual degree
at least two.  Consequently

\[
                         3s\leq N-2=P+2.                        \tag{21}
\]

With at most two distinct pencils, (21) could account for at most

\[
                       2\left\lfloor{P+2\over3}\right\rfloor   \tag{22}
\]

pool values.  This is strictly smaller than \(P\) for every \(P\geq6\).
The assumed rank four is impossible, proving

\[
                         \boxed{\operatorname{rank}
                              \Phi_{\cal K}\leq3}.              \tag{23}
\]

This rank collapse is not yet a contradiction.  It says precisely that
the quotient Plücker cubic of the evaluation planes lies in a projective
plane section of the Klein quadric.  Any continuation for \(P=6,7,8\)
should classify that plane section (an \(\alpha\)-plane or a conic) and
then reinsert the exact first-order rows; comparing only the individual
square-pencil Wronskians has now been exhausted.

For the twelve residual \(C=6\) families, the exact pool sizes are

\[
\begin{array}{c|c|l}
e&P&(a,b,u)\\ \hline
0&8&(0,8,5)\\
0&7&(1,7,4)\\
0&6&(2,6,3)\\
0&5&(3,5,2)\\
0&4&(4,4,1)\\
0&2&(6,0,3),(6,1,1)\\ \hline
1&6&(0,7,3)\\
1&5&(1,6,2)\\
1&4&(2,5,1)\\
1&1&(5,0,2),(5,1,0).
\end{array}                                                    \tag{24}
\]

Here \(e\) is the number of quartic classes.  Theorem 1.1 closes exactly
the six rows with \(2\leq P\leq5\).  The two \(P=1\) profiles have no
second moving singleton and therefore lie outside any cross-choice
coupling.  Theorem 1.2 applies to the four \(P=6,7,8\) profiles.

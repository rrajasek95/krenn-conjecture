# The two-`K_4` chart needs at least two singular cross blocks

## 1. Result

Retain the two standard ternary `K_4` shores and arbitrary cross blocks

\[
                         B_{ij}\in\operatorname {Mat}_3(\mathbb C),
                         \qquad 0\le i,j<4.
\]

Let `P(a,b)` be their four-cross permanent coefficient.  Every realization
of the equality target has

\[
                         P(a,b)=0                       \tag{1}
\]

whenever `a` or `b` is one of the thirty dead shore words.  The companion
determinantal-boundary note excluded the locus on which all sixteen blocks
are invertible.  The stronger conclusion is:

**Theorem 1.1.**  Any array satisfying the left dead-slab equations (1) has
at least two singular cross blocks.  Equivalently,

\[
 \#\{(i,j):\det B_{ij}=0\}\ne0,1.                       \tag{2}
\]

This uses neither the six isolated Hamilton-cycle dead words nor any
right-shore equation.  The eight dead coordinate lines already suffice.
The exact audit is

```text
computations/verify_two_k4_two_singular_boundary.py
```

## 2. Cofactor collapse with one singular reference block

Suppose toward a contradiction that exactly one block is singular.  Shore
translations let us call it `B_00`; the other fifteen blocks are invertible.
As in
[`two-k4-dead-slice-determinantal-boundary.md`](two-k4-dead-slice-determinantal-boundary.md),
fix one of the eight dead coordinate lines, with free left coordinate `i`.
After contracting the right sites, its three equations combine into

\[
                       \sum_{j=0}^3B_{ij}y_j C_j=0,     \tag{3}
\]

where `C_j` is the complementary three-by-three vector permanent.

If `i!=0`, all four blocks in the reference row are invertible, and the
four-vector syzygy argument gives

                              C_0=C_1=C_2=C_3=0.        \tag{4}

There is only one point to check when `i=0`.  Put `d=rank B_00`.

* If `d=1` or `2`, first take the coefficients of (3) along each vector in
  `ker B_00`.  They have the form

  \[
                         x_1D_1+x_2D_2+x_3D_3=0,        \tag{5}
  \]

  for three generic three-vectors and square-free bilinear `D_j`.  This
  syzygy map is injective, so every `D_j` is zero.  Thus each `C_j` factors
  through `x_0=B_00y_0`, which ranges over a `d`-space.  The restricted
  four-vector square-free syzygy still has nullity one for `d=1,2` (as it
  does for `d=3`), generated by the restricted alternating determinant
  relation.  Its scalar cannot be nonzero, because `C_0` would make
  `Per_3` locally equivalent to `Det_3`.  Hence (4) again holds.
* If `d=0`, (3) has only the last three vector terms.  Applying the
  injectivity in (5) coefficient by coefficient in `y_0` gives

  \[
                              C_1=C_2=C_3=0,            \tag{6}
  \]

  while `C_0` is not needed below.

The checker verifies over the integers that (5) has rank `27/27`, and that
the restricted four-vector maps for `d=1,2,3` have respective ranks
`53/54`, `80/81`, and `107/108`.

## 3. A stronger zero-`Per_3` lemma

The following strengthens the rank-at-least-two observation used in the
first boundary note.

**Lemma 3.1.**  Let `A_1,A_2,A_3` be three nonzero local maps for `Per_3`,
and suppose every coordinate basis vector has nonzero image under every
`A_l`.  If

\[
                    (A_1\otimes A_2\otimes A_3)\operatorname {Per}_3=0,
                                                               \tag{7}
\]

then at least two of the three maps have rank one.

**Proof.**  The earlier rank-two lemma excludes the case in which all three
ranks are at least two.  Suppose exactly one map, say `A_1`, has rank one.
Write

\[
                 A_1e_r=\alpha_r u.
\]

Every `alpha_r` is nonzero by hypothesis.  Contracting the first factor of
`Per_3` leaves the bilinear matrix

\[
 M(\alpha)=
 \begin{pmatrix}
 0&\alpha_2&\alpha_1\\
 \alpha_2&0&\alpha_0\\
 \alpha_1&\alpha_0&0
 \end{pmatrix},
 \qquad \det M(\alpha)=2\alpha_0\alpha_1\alpha_2\ne0. \tag{8}
\]

For maps of ranks at least two in the other two factors, (7) would make two
planes totally orthogonal under this nondegenerate pairing.  Their
dimensions sum to at least four in a three-space, which is impossible.
`QED`

Consequently, if all four complementary `Per_3` tensors of a four-column
array vanish and none of its twelve displayed vectors is zero, at least
three of the four column maps have rank one: every three-subset must contain
at least two rank-one maps.

## 4. Incidence demand

A rank-one column map means exactly that the three selected rows in that
right block column are proportional.  Call this an incidence between the
oriented left triangle and the right column.

Let `z` be the number of zero rows of the exceptional block `B_00`.

* The two oriented triangles with hole `0` use only rows of the other three
  left block rows.  If `rank B_00>0`, all four cofactors vanish and Lemma 3.1
  forces at least three incidences.  If `B_00=0`, only the three triples
  containing right column `0` are known to vanish by (6).  Requiring at
  least two rank-one maps in each of these three triples still forces at
  least three incidences among the four columns.
* Among the other six oriented triangles, each colour row of `B_00` is used
  exactly twice.  If the selected row is nonzero, all four local maps have
  nonzero coordinate images, so at least three incidences are forced.  If
  it is zero, the three good right columns still have nonzero coordinate
  images, and their complementary tensor `C_0` vanishes.  Lemma 3.1 forces
  at least two incidences in those three good columns.

The total incidence demand is therefore

\[
                  3(8-2z)+2(2z)=24-2z.                 \tag{9}
\]

For ranks two, one, and zero, respectively, this is at least

\[
                         22,\qquad20,\qquad18.          \tag{10}
\]

## 5. Incidence capacity

At a good right column `j!=0`, the three rows belonging to each left vertex
come from an invertible block and are projectively distinct.  Among the
eight oriented-triangle equalities, at most four can hold simultaneously.
This is the `2^8` union-find table

\[
                         (1,8,16,8,2)                  \tag{11}
\]

for zero through four equalities; there are no valid larger subsets.
Thus the three good columns have total capacity twelve.

At the exceptional column, the same finite table is run with the actual row
matroid of `B_00`.

* A rank-two three-by-three matrix has either three distinct nonzero row
  points, one proportional pair, or one zero row and two distinct points.
  In all cases the triangle capacity is at most four.
* For rank one, all nonzero rows are one projective point and the other rows
  may vanish.  The capacity is at most two.  It is also two for the zero
  matrix, because only the two triangles with hole zero can occur.

Hence total capacity is at most sixteen in rank two and fourteen in ranks
one and zero.  These bounds contradict (10), proving Theorem 1.1.

## 6. Audit of the earlier seven-binomial certificate

The all-invertible note retained a longer independent closure through the
six Hamilton dead words.  Its seven-binomial certificate is sound but is no
longer needed for the sharper incidence count above.  The only divisions in
that argument are by the row scalars `s_icj`.  They are nonzero because all
sixteen blocks are assumed invertible and each chosen projective frame row
is nonzero.  No cofactor, determinant syzygy scalar, or target coefficient
is divided by.  Equivalently, the checker verifies the integer Laurent
exponent relation before any division; clearing the seven nonzero monomial
denominators gives the same contradiction.

## 7. Remaining boundary

The full two-`K_4` chart is not yet excluded.  What remains begins on the
codimension-two union

\[
             \bigcup_{(i,j)\ne(k,l)}
             \{\det B_{ij}=\det B_{kl}=0\}.             \tag{12}
\]

The next natural split is whether two singular blocks share a block row or
column, lie on a matching, or already force a cross graph of matching number
at most three after row/column support propagation.

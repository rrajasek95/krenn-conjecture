# The three-copy alternating invariant

Let `V=C^3` with ordered basis `e_0,e_1,e_2`, and let
`epsilon in (V^*)^(tensor 3)` be the alternating form normalized by
`epsilon(e_0,e_1,e_2)=1`.  For `T in V^(tensor B)` define

\[
 I_B(T)=\epsilon^{\otimes B}(T,T,T).                        \tag{1}
\]

Only even `|B|` is relevant.  This note gives the exact expansion of (1)
for a matching tensor, extracts its strongest safe existential consequence,
and identifies why alternating-cycle exchanges do not turn it into a
uniform obstruction.

## 1. Target value and coefficient form

For colorings `c:B -> {0,1,2}`, write `T_c` for the corresponding
coefficient.  Expanding (1) gives

\[
 I_B(T)=
 \sum_{\substack{c^1,c^2,c^3\\
        (c_v^1,c_v^2,c_v^3)\text{ is a permutation at every }v}}
 \left(\prod_{v\in B}\epsilon_{c_v^1c_v^2c_v^3}\right)
 T_{c^1}T_{c^2}T_{c^3}.                                    \tag{2}
\]

For `Delta_(B,3)=sum_r e_r^(tensor B)`, only the six global permutations
of the three constant colorings survive.  Hence

\[
 I_B(\Delta_{B,3})=
 \begin{cases}
 6,&|B|\text{ even},\\
 0,&|B|\text{ odd}.
 \end{cases}                                                \tag{3}
\]

For odd order the form in (1) is alternating in its three identical
arguments and therefore vanishes identically.  From now on let `|B|=n` be
even.

Equation (2) also shows that the invariant is algebraically redundant after
the target coefficient equations are imposed.  Modulo all mixed
coefficients,

\[
 I_B(T)\equiv 6T_{0^n}T_{1^n}T_{2^n}.                      \tag{4}
\]

After `T_(r^n)=1`, this is exactly `6`.  More explicitly,
`I_B(T)-6` lies in the ideal generated by the mixed coefficients and the
three polynomials `T_(r^n)-1`: the mixed summands of (2) are visibly in the
first part of the ideal, and

\[
 T_{0^n}T_{1^n}T_{2^n}-1
 =(T_{0^n}-1)T_{1^n}T_{2^n}
  +(T_{1^n}-1)T_{2^n}+(T_{2^n}-1).                         \tag{5}
\]

Thus any gain must come from resolving (1) in the *source matching
parameters*, not from an additional output equation.

## 2. Exact matching expansion

Let

\[
 H_B(A)=\sum_{M\in\mathcal M(B)}\bigotimes_{e\in M}A_e,    \tag{6}
\]

where matrices are ordered by their endpoints.  For an ordered triple of
perfect matchings `M=(M_1,M_2,M_3)`, put

\[
 \begin{split}
 \Phi_A(M_1,M_2,M_3)
  =\sum_{\pi:B\to S_3}
   &\left(\prod_{v\in B}\operatorname{sgn}(\pi_v)\right)\\
   &\cdot\prod_{k=1}^3\prod_{uv\in M_k}
       A_{uv}\bigl(\pi_u(k),\pi_v(k)\bigr).
 \end{split}                                                \tag{7}
\]

Here the symbols `1,2,3` indexing the copies are identified with the three
slots permuted by `pi_v`; colors themselves remain `0,1,2`.

**Proposition 2.1 (matching-triple expansion).**

\[
 I_B(H_B(A))=
 \sum_{(M_1,M_2,M_3)\in\mathcal M(B)^3}
       \Phi_A(M_1,M_2,M_3).                                \tag{8}
\]

**Proof.**  Expand the three copies of (6), then contract at each vertex.
The local alternating form is zero unless its three colors are distinct;
writing the surviving local color assignment as `pi_v` gives (7).
Summing the ordered matching choices gives (8). `QED`

There is a more structural form.  Choose any rank factorization

\[
 A_{uv}=\sum_{s=1}^{r_{uv}}
 a_{uv,s}^{(u)}\otimes a_{uv,s}^{(v)}.                     \tag{9}
\]

For every occurrence of an edge in one of the three matchings choose a
factor label `s`.  At vertex `v`, let `a_{k,v}` be the local vector selected
on the edge of `M_k` incident to `v`.

**Proposition 2.2 (vertex-determinant expansion).**

\[
 \Phi_A(M_1,M_2,M_3)
   =\sum_{\text{factor labels}}
      \prod_{v\in B}\det[a_{1,v}\ a_{2,v}\ a_{3,v}].     \tag{10}
\]

**Proof.**  Once the factor labels are fixed, all edge contributions split
into endpoint vectors.  The sum over `pi_v in S_3` in (7) is independently
the displayed determinant at each vertex.  Multiplying over vertices and
summing the labels proves (10). `QED`

For example, if all three matchings equal `M`, Cauchy--Binet on each edge
gives the exact diagonal-triple term

\[
 \Phi_A(M,M,M)=6^{n/2}\prod_{e\in M}\det A_e.              \tag{11}
\]

This already warns that a nonzero invariant need not produce three
distinct perfect matchings: one perfect matching of invertible matrices can
support a nonzero ordered triple by itself.

## 3. The safe rainbow-triple consequence

Assume `H_B(A)=Delta_(B,3)`.  Equations (3) and (8) imply that some ordered
matching triple has nonzero `Phi`.  Expanding either (7) or (10) yields the
following two precise versions.

**Corollary 3.1 (supported rainbow triple).**  There are perfect matchings
`M_1,M_2,M_3` and permutations `pi_v in S_3` such that

\[
 A_{uv}(\pi_u(k),\pi_v(k))\ne0
 \quad\text{for every }uv\in M_k\text{ and }k=1,2,3.       \tag{12}
\]

Thus the three matching occurrences use all three endpoint colors at every
vertex.  If an underlying edge occurs `m` times among the three matchings,
its support contains `m` cells in distinct rows and distinct columns.

**Corollary 3.2 (rank-respecting decorated triple).**  For any fixed rank
factorizations (9), there is an ordered triple and a choice of factor label
on every occurrence such that

\[
 \det[a_{1,v}\ a_{2,v}\ a_{3,v}]\ne0\qquad(v\in B).       \tag{13}
\]

In particular, an edge occurring `m` times has matrix rank at least `m`.
No rank-one edge can occur twice; if every used edge has rank one, the three
matchings are edge-disjoint at every vertex and form a simple cubic
three-edge-colored graph whose three local factor vectors are bases.

These are the strongest safe existential conclusions.  They do not say
that the matchings are globally distinct when higher-rank matrices occur.
Separately, the three normalized constant coefficients let one choose a
perfect matching supported on `(r,r)` entries for each constant color `r`,
but that coordinate-supported triple need not be the determinant-nonzero
triple supplied by (13).

## 4. Alternating exchanges preserve the contribution

Let `C` be a union of alternating-cycle components of `M_1 union M_2`, and
let `M_1',M_2'` be obtained by exchanging the two matching colors on `C`.

**Lemma 4.1 (even-cycle invariance).**

\[
 \Phi_A(M_1',M_2',M_3)=\Phi_A(M_1,M_2,M_3).                \tag{14}
\]

**Proof.**  Use (10).  Exchange the factor labels together with their edge
occurrences on `C`.  This is a bijection between the two factor-label sums.
At every vertex of `C`, columns one and two of the local determinant are
swapped and contribute a sign `-1`.  Every alternating cycle has even
length, so the product of these signs is `+1`.  All other local
determinants are unchanged. `QED`

The same proof covers a global permutation of the three matching copies:
for even `n`, the sign is `sgn(sigma)^n=1`.  Consequently alternating-cycle
orbits reinforce a nonzero contribution by equal copies; they do not give a
sign-reversing cancellation.

In the all-rank-one case, Corollary 3.2 produces a properly three-edge-
colored cubic graph.  Standard alternating-cycle/chord arguments give a
fourth perfect matching when `n>=6`.  Lemma 4.1, however, shows that switches
among the original factorization contribute the same `Phi`; it supplies no
equation forcing that common value to vanish.  A fourth matching outside a
chosen switch orbit likewise has no coefficientwise uniqueness in the full
complete-graph matching sum.

## 5. Exact localization gap

For a coloring `c`, write

\[
 w_M(c)=\prod_{uv\in M}A_{uv}(c_u,c_v),\qquad
 H_B(A)_c=\sum_Mw_M(c).                                    \tag{15}
\]

If `c` is mixed, the target equation says that the *whole* sum in (15) is
zero.  In (2), the matching sums for a fixed triple of colorings factor as

\[
 \sum_{M_1,M_2,M_3}\prod_{k=1}^3w_{M_k}(c^k)
   =\prod_{k=1}^3H_B(A)_{c^k}.                              \tag{16}
\]

This vanishes when one `c^k` is mixed, but only after summing the full
Cartesian product of all perfect matchings.  An alternating-cycle orbit is
in general a proper subset of that Cartesian product.  Lemma 4.1 says its
already-color-summed contribution is constant on the orbit rather than
opposite in sign.  Therefore mixed-coefficient vanishing cannot be applied
termwise, or even orbitwise, to the nonzero triple in Corollary 3.2.

There is a small exact illustration of this failure at the CP level which
does not merely add a tensor and its negative.  Put

\[
 a=e_0+e_1+e_2,\qquad R=a^{\otimes(n-1)}.
\]

Distributivity gives the three-term zero relation

\[
 (e_0+e_2)\otimes R-e_0\otimes R-e_2\otimes R=0.           \tag{17}
\]

Add (17) to the three standard CP summands of `Delta_(n,3)`.  All mixed
coefficients of the total tensor still vanish.  Nevertheless, the ordered
triple formed by `e_0^(tensor n)`, `e_1^(tensor n)`, and
`(e_0+e_2) tensor R` has alternating contribution

\[
 \det[e_0,e_1,e_0+e_2]\prod_{v=2}^n\det[e_0,e_1,a]=1.      \tag{18}
\]

Its cancellation uses the other two, genuinely distinct CP summands in
(17).  This is not asserted to be a matching-tensor counterexample; it is
an exact counterexample to the logical step that mixed vanishing kills an
individual rainbow triple.  Any successful matching-specific continuation
would need a new theorem forcing all cancellations of (16) to remain inside
a chosen alternating orbit.  Neither the invariant nor cycle exchange
provides such a theorem.

## 6. Conclusion

The three-copy invariant yields the rigorous rank-respecting rainbow triple
of Corollaries 3.1--3.2.  It does not by itself give a uniform contradiction
for `n>=6`: repeated high-rank edges are allowed by (11), and in the
rank-one case alternating exchanges preserve contributions while mixed
coefficient equations cancel only after the unrestricted matching sums in
(16).  The precise missing input is an orbit-localization result, and the
CP identity (17) shows that such localization cannot follow from target
coefficient vanishing alone.
